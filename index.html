Evaluation of Explainable AI

My PhD research focuses on the evaluation of explainable AI (XAI) methods (often applied to time series data). I am interested in how we can reliably assess the (computational) correctness of explanations, and how that may (or may not) relate to human outcomes such as understanding.

Class-dependent evaluation effects

Perturbation analysis is often used to evaluate how "correct" or "faithful" explanations are. It's also unclear, however, how reliable perturbation metrics are as their results can depend a lot on how you perturb the data. In the below projects, we studied how sensitive perturbation metrics can be to dataset-specific characteristics like class membership.

Class-dependent perturbation effects in evaluating time-series attributions

xAI 2025 · Baer, Grau, Zhang, Van Gorp

We evaluated feature attribution methods for time series classification across datasets, model architectures, and perturbation strategies. We found that the strongest perturbation strategies often showed the largest class differences.

This is troubling, as we don't know a priori what "good" perturbations are, or which classes are sensitive to them. Nor do we know whether explanations for the insensitive classes are actually bad, or whether the evaluation approach itself does not fit those classes.

Why do class-dependent evaluation effects occur?

ECML PKDD 2025 · Baer, Grau, Zhang, Van Gorp

We investigated why class-dependent evaluation effects occur by generating synthetic time series with known feature locations with varying feature types and class differences. We contrasted perturbation with localization (ground-truth) metrics to see how they differ and where the effects come from.

The class differences appeared even in these simple, controlled settings, where differences between classes were only their amplitude or duration. This suggests we should be careful in interpreting evaluation results, especially so when localization and perturbation metrics can also disagree about which class is explained "better".

Computational metrics and human understanding

Computational evaluation metrics can be useful to test whether explanations are "correct" in a technical sense. In the end though, we may want to know whether explanations are useful to humans. This project studies how computational explanation correctness relates to whether people can understand AI decision patterns.

Linking Computational XAI Evaluation to Human Understanding

Preprint 2026 · Baer, Zhang, Grau, Van Gorp

We recruited participants in an online user study to predict an AI system's decisions in a time series classification task with feature attribution-style explanations. Whether participants could predict the AI's decisions functioned as a proxy for one component of their understanding of the explanations. We manipulated the correctness of the explanations at four levels, to see whether less correct explanations would lead to worse understanding as measured by forward simulation accuracy.

In our experiments, correctness did affect participants understanding (prediction accuracy), but not equally across all levels of correctness. Even with fully correct explanations, some participants had trouble understanding the AI's decision patterns, while others could predict almost perfectly. Self-reported verbalized understanding from participants also showed some mismatch between perceived, verbalized understanding and actual task performance.

While computational correctness metrics are useful as a first evaluation step, we should be careful in assuming that higher correctness will automatically translate to better human understanding.

Tools for controlled evaluation

xaitimesynth is a small Python package for easily generating synthetic time-series for evaluating XAI methods against ground truth data using localization metrics.

xaitimesynth: A Python Package for Evaluating Attribution Methods

Python package · 2026

xaitimesynth creates datasets with known feature locations, so attribution methods can be tested in controlled and reproducible settings against localization metrics. Time series are built by combining a background signal with localized, class-discriminating features, in univariate or multivariate form. The feature locations are kept as ground-truth masks to test whether attribution methods highlight the right time points.

The package is designed to more easily generate synthetic time series, when you want to keep track of feature locations, and easily distribute the locations with a high level API across time series channels and classes. It includes standard localization metrics such as AUC-PR, AUC-ROC, Relevance Mass Accuracy, and Relevance Rank Accuracy.
